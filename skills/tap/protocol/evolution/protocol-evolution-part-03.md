**Mid-reset refusal text (tap-2oq5h):** a verb refused by the reset fence names the fence path and selects its next step from the fence owner's observed liveness — `wait for the reset owner (or until identity is readable), then: tap team reset` while the owner is live, and `tap team reset  # retry completes the cut, then restore` once it is PROVED stale. The two say opposite things to the operator, so the choice is part of the contract rather than presentation. **A record that names no owner is a third outcome (tap-mkiqq).** An unreadable fence record (not JSON, or not an object), or one missing `pid`, `boot_id` or `owner_start`, still resolves to LIVE for every reclaim decision below. Its refusal, though, says neither mid-reset nor wait, because no owner exists to finish and nothing will ever reclaim it. The refusal names the record `is unreadable` or `names no owner (pid, boot_id or owner_start missing)`, states that nothing will clear it automatically, and offers `inspect <path> and, once you are sure no reset is running, remove it: rm <path>`, with the path POSIX-quoted when the shell would split it. The acquire path never writes such a record. The launcher doors (`session` launch, resume, restart and repair), which fence on presence alone, use the same text in both runtimes. A native mutator that cannot acquire the reset sidecar but observes no reset fence refuses instead with `error_class=reset_custody_busy` and a retry of its own operation; sidecar contention alone never recommends `tap team reset`. Proof of staleness requires a complete three-factor stamp — recorded boot id, pid, and process-start fingerprint — and any unreadable factor (missing stamp field, unreadable current boot, a `ps` that will not answer) resolves to LIVE: "I could not tell" never licenses reclaiming a fence somebody may still hold. A live pid whose start fingerprint no longer matches is stale, which is what keeps pid reuse from reading as continued ownership. The refusal's detail names the caller's full verb (`refusing tap poll — …`). **The boot comparison spans identity schemes rather than requiring one, and it has THREE outcomes.** A recorded `boot:<sec>` stamp read on a host whose current identity is `bootuuid:<uuid>` is compared against the host's own independently observed legacy boot epoch with a five-second window, so a legacy stamp from the current boot proves the owner LIVE while one from a prior boot proves it STALE even when the recorded pid is alive. Exact UUIDs stay exact. The third outcome is **UNKNOWN**: when the two stamps cannot be related at all — a legacy row whose secondary oracle will not answer, or an exact row against a legacy current — the boot factor joins the other unreadable factors above and resolves to **LIVE**, never to stale. Both cross-scheme directions resolve the same way. Collapsing unknown into mismatch would let a fence whose owner is alive be reclaimed on the strength of a comparison nobody could make, which is the rule's first sentence read backwards. An explicit `TAP_BOOT_ID` without `TAP_BOOT_ID_LEGACY` keeps exact comparison, so a reboot fixture does not silently become a tolerance fixture. Both runtimes select identically. **Unreadability is decided on SHAPE, before anything is inferred from a value.** Only two stamp shapes are recognized, the two `tap_boot_id` emits — `boot:<digits>` and `bootuuid:` followed by lowercase hex in 8-4-4-4-12 groups. A recorded or current stamp in any other shape (`garbage`, or `bootuuid:not-a-uuid`, which carries the right prefix and is still not an id) is UNKNOWN and resolves to LIVE; it is never a positive mismatch, because "this is not a boot id" says nothing about which boot wrote it. The same rule governs the pid factor: the recorded pid must be a positive integer, and `0`, negatives, and non-numeric values are unreadable rather than evidence — `0` and negatives address a process GROUP, so a liveness probe on them answers a different question than the one asked and has been observed to return success for a stamp that is not a pid at all. Every reset-fence door now uses the native predicate in `runtime/bun/src/reset.ts`: Bash adapters, native readers and reset acquire share one decision. `process.kill(pid, 0)` proves absence only through `ESRCH`, which resolves to STALE without consulting `ps`. `EPERM` withholds proof of death and continues to the process-start fingerprint; every other error resolves to LIVE. The former Bash-only process-table corroboration is retired. Consequently, a dead owner produces the retry action on both doors even when `ps` is unavailable; an occupied owner whose fingerprint cannot be read still resolves to LIVE. **Shape is decided before equality, not after.** Two stamps that are byte-equal but in no recognized shape are UNKNOWN rather than MATCH, because MATCH does not end the evaluation — it hands the decision on to the pid and start-fingerprint factors, so an equality that proves nothing about which boot wrote the stamp would let a later dead pid authorize the reclaim. UNKNOWN short-circuits to LIVE; MATCH does not. **Permission denied withholds proof of death; it does not supply proof of life.** An `EPERM` signal probe continues to the process-start fingerprint; an unreadable fingerprint resolves to LIVE. An unsignallable pid whose recorded fingerprint is not its own is STALE — otherwise a recycled pid under another uid would hold a fence permanently and no cut could ever complete. **The recorded start fingerprint is an instant, not a rendering.** `ps -o lstart=` renders in the reading process's timezone, so a fence written under one zone and read under another compared different text for one process and manufactured positive proof of staleness; the fence records seconds since the epoch, produced and compared timezone-free. A recorded fingerprint that is not a canonical integer is a legacy rendering that cannot be attributed to any zone, and it resolves to LIVE rather than being guessed at — a reset fence is transient, so that covers a cut already in flight across an upgrade. **Digits are not an epoch, and a leading zero is not a smaller number.** A stamp is readable only if it is the CANONICAL rendering of a value the three implementations can represent identically: either the single digit `0`, or a first digit of `1`-`9` followed by at most fourteen more. A leading zero is unreadable — the boot factor is compared numerically, and the same stamp reads as its plain value in JavaScript and Python while the shell enters OCTAL arithmetic, where `8` and `9` are not digits at all; the expansion fails, the failure resolves to "differ", and the shell reclaims a fence the other two hold. That is the one direction in this predicate where the incumbent takes a fence the ports protect. **The canonical-shape rule governs every numeric identity factor** — the legacy boot epoch, the recorded start fingerprint, and the recorded pid alike. **The two bounds beneath it are different, and the subject decides which applies.** For the two EPOCH factors the bound is a digit count: at most fifteen, which keeps every accepted value below 2^53 and therefore exact in a JavaScript double, a 64-bit shell integer and a Python integer at once. For the PID the bound is the process API range, `1` through `2147483647`, and a digit count would not do: `2147483648` is ten digits and still outside what a process id can hold. The pid also keeps a narrower canonical shape than the epochs — it must be a POSITIVE integer, because `0` and a leading zero address a process GROUP, so a liveness probe on them answers a different question and has been observed to succeed for a stamp that is not a pid, whereas `0` is a legitimate epoch value and stays readable for the other two factors. An out-of-range pid is unanswerable by BOTH probes while each fails toward reclaim — a signal check overflows and reports success without entering the process-table corroboration at all, while the process table exits no-match with empty output, which that corroborator reads as proof of absence.
